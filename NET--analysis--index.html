INFERENCE The last two columns convert projected marks into printed questions, which is what a candidate actually meets. A topic’s projected marks are split between the tiers in the proportion it has been asked in historically, then divided by the tier’s printed marks and grossed up by the tier’s answer ratio — 50 printed for 35 answered in Part B, 80 for 25 in Part C. The two columns therefore sum to 50 and 80, the paper’s own counts.

SPECULATIVE Treat a projected figure as the centre of a range about half as wide again, not as a number. Across the corpus the typical topic’s single-year weight sits anywhere from half to twice its own average, and nothing in this method narrows that.

INFERENCE Two facts about the marking that should shape how the paper is attempted. The penalty is not the same in the two subject parts: the papers themselves print 0.5 against a 2-mark question and 1.32 against a 4-mark one, which is 25% in Part B and 33% in Part C. So a blind guess among four options is worth exactly zero in Parts A and B and −0.33 marks in Part C — guessing is free in the two-mark tier and costs in the four-mark tier, and in Part C only an elimination makes it worth attempting. And Part C questions are worth twice Part B ones but a candidate answers only 25 of the 80: the binding constraint there is choosing which 25, not answering them.

INFERENCE Three hours for 75 answered questions is 2.4 minutes each, but the paper is not answered evenly: 15 aptitude questions and 35 Part B questions are mostly recall and go faster, which is what buys the time for the 25 Part C questions where the four marks are.

SPECULATIVE No projection of individual Part A heads is offered, and that is a considered omission.
