Predicted question papers for UPSC Combined Geo-Scientist 2027 (Geologist)

A complete cycle: both prelims papers — General Studies (120 items, 100 marks) and Geology/Hydrogeology (120 items, 300 marks) — and three mains papers of 200 marks each. Every question is constructed to the topic weightage projected in report 5 and report 11, and to the recurrences documented in report 8. Answers and the evidential basis for each question are given inline.

SPECULATIVE Read this first. Nothing here is a leaked or actual UPSC question. Each item is written by working backwards from what the 2014–2026 papers have actually asked: the topic allocation is arithmetic from the projection, and the individual questions are the concepts that recur most often, re-asked in the moulds the recent papers favour. Treat these papers as a revision instrument — a way to rehearse the right concepts under the right marks pressure and the right negative marking — not as a prediction of the questions that will be set.

EVIDENCE Prelims Paper I is included because it is a gate, not a formality: a candidate who misses its cut-off never sits the mains papers at all, even with a perfect Paper II. Its marks do not enter the final ranking, which is exactly why it is the paper most often under-prepared.

1. How the papers were allocated

INFERENCE Geology first. The projection in report 5 gives each topic a share of the 900 Geology marks a full cycle offers. That share is split between the stages in the ratio the stages themselves carry — 300 prelims marks against 600 mains marks — and the prelims figure is divided by 2.5, the marks per item, to give an item count. Rounding to whole items loses two of the 120, and rounding the marks columns gains three, which is left visible in the totals rather than smoothed away.

EVIDENCE The last column is what was actually written, and six topics depart from the arithmetic by one or two items. Mineral Exploration and Hydrogeology gained two each because the 2024–2026 papers have asked several drilling, geophysical-method and aquifer-arithmetic items worth setting in full; Stratigraphy and Geochemistry gained one each to absorb the two items rounding had lost; Economic Geology gave up two, and Remote Sensing and Environmental Geology one each. Every deviation is inside the rounding noise of the projection, and is recorded here rather than hidden.

EVIDENCE The paper structures are UPSC's own and have not changed since 2016. Prelims Paper II: 120 items of 2.5 marks, one-third deducted for a wrong answer, two hours. Each mains paper: eleven questions in six sections, of which a candidate answers six — the compulsory Section A question worth 50 and one 30-mark question from each of Sections B to F — in three hours. The mains papers below print all eleven questions as the real ones do, so their parts sum to about 350 while a script is worth 200.

INFERENCE Item moulds are labelled by the same wording-based classifier that produced report 7, applied to these constructed stems rather than asserted by hand. The result: 82% direct recall, 6% statement sets, 5% not-items, 3% list-matching, 3% single-correct discriminations, 1% numerical. Against the historical mix that is heavier on direct recall — 53% in the real papers — and lighter on statement sets (10% historically) and list-matching (6%). A written-to-order item tends to state its question plainly, so read those two moulds as under-represented here and drill more of them from the real papers.

EVIDENCE Items run in the order the real papers use — surface processes, structure, stratigraphy and palaeontology, then petrology and mineralogy, then the applied subjects.

2. Prelims Paper I: General Studies

EVIDENCE 120 items · 0.83 marks each · 100 marks · two hours · 0.28 deducted for each wrong answer. This paper is qualifying only — its marks do not count towards the final rank — but failing its cut-off ends the attempt.

Geography (items 1–20)

1

Single correctGeography

Which one of the following is the least abundant by volume in dry air?

  1. Argon
  2. Carbon dioxide
  3. Neon
  4. Oxygen

Answer: C. Dry air is 78.1% N2, 20.9% O2, 0.93% Ar, 0.04% CO2 and about 0.0018% Ne. The order N > O > Ar > CO2 > Ne is worth memorising, because the paper asks it in both directions.

Basis: the 2021 and 2025 papers each asked for the highest or least abundant atmospheric constituent.

2

Single correctGeography

Temperature inversion is most frequently observed

  1. on a windy afternoon over a plain
  2. on a clear, calm night in a valley
  3. during heavy monsoon rainfall
  4. over a warm ocean current

Answer: B. Clear skies allow strong radiative cooling of the ground, calm air prevents mixing, and cold dense air drains into the valley floor — so temperature rises with height. Wind or cloud destroys the inversion, which is why it is a night-time, fair-weather phenomenon and why valley towns trap smog on winter mornings.

Basis: asked in the 2021 paper.

3

Single correctGeography

Which one of the following pairs of local wind and region is correctly matched?

  1. Chinook — the Alps
  2. Harmattan — West Africa
  3. Foehn — the Rockies
  4. Loo — the Mediterranean

Answer: B. The Harmattan is the dry dusty north-easterly that blows off the Sahara across West Africa. Chinook belongs to the Rockies and Foehn to the Alps — the two are deliberately swapped in options A and C — and the Loo is the hot summer wind of northern India.

Basis: the 2025 paper asked which local wind is called the ‘Doctor's wind’; local winds appear in five of seven years.

Statement setGeography

Consider the following statements regarding the south-west monsoon in India:

  1. It normally sets in over Kerala in the first week of June.
  2. The Arabian Sea branch and the Bay of Bengal branch advance independently.
  3. Its withdrawal begins from north-west India.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

  1. 1 and 2 only
  2. 2 and 3 only
  3. 1 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3

Answer: D. All three hold. Onset over Kerala is dated 1 June on average; the two branches follow separate tracks and meet over the north-western plains; and withdrawal starts in north-west India in September and retreats south-eastwards — the reverse of the advance.

Basis: the 2026 paper set a statement item on the south-west monsoon.

Single correctGeography

The Kumaon Himalaya lies between which two rivers?

  1. Indus and Satluj
  2. Satluj and Kali
  3. Kali and Tista
  4. Tista and Dihang

Answer: B. The conventional west-to-east divisions are Punjab Himalaya (Indus–Satluj), Kumaon Himalaya (Satluj–Kali), Nepal Himalaya (Kali–Tista) and Assam Himalaya (Tista–Dihang). The four options are the four consecutive segments, so the item is only answerable if the whole sequence is known.

Basis: asked in the 2023 paper.

Single correctGeography

An entrepot port is best defined as one that

  1. handles only bulk cargo
  2. imports goods mainly for re-export rather than domestic use
  3. lies at the mouth of a navigable river
  4. serves a single industrial hinterland

Answer: B. An entrepot collects, stores and redistributes goods bound elsewhere, so its trade is transhipment rather than consumption. Singapore, Rotterdam and Dubai are the classic examples; the model needs a location on a major shipping route plus liberal customs treatment, not a large local market.

Basis: asked in the 2021 paper.

7

Single correctGeography

Urban sprawl is said to occur when

  1. population density in the city core rises sharply
  2. built-up area expands outwards faster than the population grows
  3. a city's administrative boundary is redrawn
  4. rural migrants settle within the existing city limits

Answer: B. Sprawl is low-density outward growth: land consumption outruns population growth, so per-capita infrastructure cost rises and farmland on the fringe is lost. Densification, option A, is its opposite.

Basis: asked in the 2021 paper.

Single correctGeography

Which one of the following States is the largest producer of natural gas in India?

  1. Gujarat
  2. Rajasthan
  3. Assam
  4. Tripura

Answer: C. Among States, Assam leads on onshore natural gas, though the largest single producing field in the country is offshore Mumbai High, which belongs to no State. Distinguishing onshore State output from total national output is the trap the item sets.

Basis: asked in the 2023 paper.

Single correctGeography

Little Andaman is separated from Great Andaman by

  1. the Ten Degree Channel
  2. Duncan Passage
  3. the Nine Degree Channel
  4. the Coco Strait

Answer: B. Duncan Passage separates Little Andaman from South Andaman. The Ten Degree Channel divides the Andamans from the Nicobars, the Nine Degree Channel divides Minicoy from the rest of Lakshadweep, and the Coco Strait separates the Andamans from Myanmar's Coco Islands.

Single correctGeography

The area under ravines is largest in which one of the following States?

  1. Rajasthan
  2. Madhya Pradesh
  3. Uttar Pradesh
  4. Gujarat

Answer: B. The Chambal badlands of Madhya Pradesh carry the largest ravine area, with Uttar Pradesh second. Ravines form by headward gully erosion in deep unconsolidated alluvium where vegetation has been stripped — a land-degradation problem, not a natural landform to be admired.

Basis: asked in the 2023 paper.

Single correctGeography

Which one of the following is a lagoon?

  1. Wular Lake
  2. Chilika Lake
  3. Sambhar Lake
  4. Loktak Lake

Answer: B. Chilika is a brackish coastal lagoon cut off from the Bay of Bengal by a sand bar. Wular is a tectonic–fluvial lake, Sambhar a saline playa in an arid basin, and Loktak a freshwater lake famous for its floating phumdis.

14

Single correctGeography

Which one of the following soils is transported and deposited chiefly by gravity?

  1. Alluvial soil
  2. Loess
  3. Colluvial soil
  4. Lacustrine soil

Answer: C. Colluvium accumulates at the base of a slope by creep, slide and rainwash, so it is poorly sorted and angular. Alluvium is water-borne, loess wind-borne, and lacustrine soil settles in still water.

Basis: asked in the 2023 paper.

Statement setGeography

With reference to tropical thorn forests in India, consider the following statements:

  1. They occur where annual rainfall is below about 70 cm.
  2. Trees are widely spaced and largely leafless for much of the year.
  3. They are found in parts of Rajasthan, Gujarat and the interior Deccan.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

  1. 1 and 2 only
  2. 2 and 3 only
  3. 1 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3

Answer: D. All three describe the type: low and erratic rainfall, xerophytic adaptation with long roots, thick bark, thorns and small waxy leaves shed in the dry season, and a distribution across the arid west and the rain-shadow interior.

Basis: asked in the 2026 paper.

16

Statement setGeography

Consider the following statements regarding the salinity of oceans:

  1. Salinity is generally highest in the sub-tropical high-pressure belts.
  2. The North Sea records low salinity because of freshwater inflow.
  3. Salinity generally decreases towards the poles.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

  1. 1 and 2 only
  2. 2 and 3 only
  3. 1 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3

Answer: D. High evaporation and low rainfall in the sub-tropical belts maximise salinity around 20–30° latitude; river discharge dilutes enclosed seas like the North Sea and the Baltic; and polar water is freshened by ice-melt and low evaporation. Note that the Red Sea, not the open ocean, holds the salinity record.

Basis: asked in the 2026 paper.

17

Single correctGeography

Which one of the following ocean currents is a cold current?

  1. Gulf Stream
  2. Kuroshio
  3. Benguela
  4. Brazil Current

Answer: C. The Benguela flows equatorwards along south-west Africa and, with the Peru current, drives coastal upwelling, high productivity and coastal desert. The rule worth carrying: currents flowing away from the Equator are warm, those flowing towards it along western continental margins are cold.

Single correctGeography

Roughly conical hills composed of sorted glacio-fluvial deposits are called

  1. drumlins
  2. kames
  3. eskers
  4. roches moutonnées

Answer: B. Kames are mounds of stratified sand and gravel dumped by meltwater at an ice margin. Drumlins are streamlined till mounds shaped beneath moving ice, eskers are sinuous ridges deposited in subglacial tunnels, and a roche moutonnée is abraded bedrock.

Physics (items 21–38)

Single correctPhysics

A rigid body resting on a horizontal table is in stable equilibrium if, when slightly disturbed, its centre of gravity

  1. falls
  2. rises
  3. remains at the same height
  4. moves horizontally only

Answer: B. Stable equilibrium means the disturbance raises the centre of gravity, so gravity supplies a restoring torque and the body returns. A falling centre of gravity gives unstable equilibrium; an unchanged height gives neutral equilibrium, as with a sphere on a plane.

Basis: asked in the 2026 paper.

22

NumericalPhysics

A block of wood floats in water with three-quarters of its volume submerged. Its density is

  1. 250 kg m−3
  2. 500 kg m−3
  3. 750 kg m−3
  4. 1333 kg m−3

Answer: C. Floating means weight equals buoyancy, so ρbodyV g = ρwater(3V/4)g, giving ρbody = 0.75 × 1000 = 750 kg m−3. The submerged fraction is the relative density — the single fact this item family always turns on.

Basis: the 2026 paper asked a two-liquid version of this calculation.

23

Single correctPhysics

A Geiger–Müller tube is normally used to detect

  1. magnetic field strength
  2. ionising radiation
  3. ultrasonic waves
  4. infrared radiation

Answer: B. Radiation entering the tube ionises the fill gas; the avalanche produces a countable pulse. It counts events well but discriminates energy poorly, which is why a scintillation counter is preferred for spectrometry — relevant to a geologist handling radiometric surveys.

Basis: asked in the 2026 paper.

24

Single correctPhysics

A spacecraft parked at a Lagrange point of the Sun–Earth system

  1. experiences no gravitational force at all
  2. moves with the same orbital period as the Earth
  3. must fire its engines continuously to stay there
  4. is outside the solar system

Answer: B. At a Lagrange point the combined gravity of Sun and Earth plus the centrifugal effect lets a small body keep station with the Earth, so its period matches Earth's year. Gravity is not zero — it is balanced. India's Aditya-L1 uses L1 for exactly this reason.

Basis: asked in the 2026 paper.

Statement setPhysics

If the electrical conductivity of the Earth's outer core were decreased, which of the following would be affected?

  1. The strength of the geomagnetic field.
  2. The rate of geomagnetic reversals.
  3. The Earth's rate of rotation.

Select the correct answer using the code given below.

  1. 1 and 2 only
  2. 2 and 3 only
  3. 1 and 3 only
  4. 1, 2 and 3

Answer: A. The geodynamo needs a conducting fluid in motion; weaken the conductivity and both field strength and reversal behaviour change. Rotation rate is set by angular momentum, not by conductivity.

Basis: asked in the 2026 paper. This is the kind of item where a geologist has an advantage over other candidates — it is Paper I in form and Paper II in content.

Single correctPhysics

The working of an optical fibre depends principally on

  1. diffraction
  2. dispersion
  3. total internal reflection
  4. polarisation

Answer: C. Light in the denser core strikes the core–cladding boundary beyond the critical angle and is reflected without loss, so it is guided along the fibre. Total internal reflection requires travel from denser to rarer medium at an angle greater than the critical angle — the same condition that makes a mirage.

27

Single correctPhysics

Sound cannot travel through

  1. steel
  2. water
  3. air
  4. vacuum

Answer: D. Sound is a mechanical wave and needs a material medium; it travels fastest in solids because of their high elastic modulus. The same reasoning explains why S-waves are absent from the Earth's outer core — not vacuum, but a liquid with no shear strength.

NumericalPhysics

A body is thrown vertically upwards with a speed of 20 m s−1. Taking g = 10 m s−2, the maximum height reached is

  1. 10 m
  2. 20 m
  3. 40 m
  4. 200 m

Answer: B. h = u²/2g = 400/20 = 20 m. The corresponding time up is u/g = 2 s, and total flight 4 s — both worth knowing since the paper asks either.

Single correctPhysics

Which one of the following statements about a transformer is correct?

  1. It works on direct current.
  2. It changes voltage but not frequency.
  3. It increases power output.
  4. It converts electrical energy into mechanical energy.

Answer: B. A transformer relies on a changing flux, so it needs alternating current, and it steps voltage up or down while leaving the frequency untouched. Power out cannot exceed power in — a step-up in voltage is paid for by a fall in current.

30

Single correctPhysics

The unit of magnetic flux density is

  1. weber
  2. tesla
  3. henry
  4. gauss-oersted

Answer: B. Tesla is flux density (weber per square metre); weber is total flux; henry is inductance. The Earth's field is about 25–65 microtesla, which is why geophysical magnetometers report in nanotesla.

Single correctPhysics

A convex lens held in air behaves as a diverging lens when it is immersed in a medium whose refractive index is

  1. less than that of the lens
  2. greater than that of the lens
  3. equal to that of the lens
  4. equal to that of air

Answer: B. The lens-maker's formula depends on the relative refractive index. If the surrounding medium is optically denser than the glass, the sign of the focal length reverses and the convex lens diverges; if the indices match, the lens disappears optically.

Which one of the following is not a vector quantity?

  1. Displacement
  2. Torque
  3. Work
  4. Angular momentum

Answer: C. Work is the scalar product of force and displacement, so it has magnitude only. Torque is a cross product and angular momentum a moment of momentum — both vectors.

Single correctPhysics

The dimensional formula of pressure is

  1. [ML2T−2]
  2. [ML−1T−2]
  3. [MLT−2]
  4. [ML−1T−1]

Heat transfer through a vacuum flask's silvered walls is reduced chiefly by minimising

  1. conduction
  2. convection
  3. radiation
  4. evaporation

Answer: C. Silvering reflects thermal radiation; the vacuum between the walls is what kills conduction and convection. Each feature of the flask defeats one mode of transfer, and the paper likes asking which does which.

Single correctPhysics

Isotopes of an element differ in the number of

  1. protons
  2. electrons
  3. neutrons
  4. valence electrons

Answer: C. Same atomic number, different mass number. This is the fact underlying every radiometric dating method in the geology syllabus, so it belongs to both papers.

Single correctPhysics

Which one of the following electromagnetic radiations has the longest wavelength?

  1. X-rays
  2. Ultraviolet
  3. Visible light
  4. Microwaves

Answer: D. In increasing wavelength: gamma, X-ray, ultraviolet, visible, infrared, microwave, radio. Remote sensing uses the infrared and microwave ends precisely because they behave differently from visible light in cloud and vegetation.

NumericalPhysics

The equivalent resistance of two 6 Ω resistors connected in parallel is

  1. 12 Ω
  2. 6 Ω
  3. 3 Ω
  4. 1.5 Ω

Answer: C. For two equal resistances in parallel the result is half of one, so 3 Ω. In series it would be 12 Ω — the two cases are the whole of what this item family tests.

38

Single correctPhysics

The phenomenon responsible for the blue colour of the clear sky is

  1. reflection
  2. refraction
  3. scattering
  4. interference

Answer: C. Rayleigh scattering varies as 1/λ4, so short blue wavelengths scatter far more than red. At sunset the longer path scatters the blue away entirely, leaving red — the same physics, opposite outcome.

Single correctBiology

When Paramecium caudatum and Paramecium aurelia are cultured together under ideal laboratory conditions with a single limiting resource, the usual outcome is

  1. both persist at equal densities
  2. one species is eliminated
  3. both go extinct
  4. they hybridise

Answer: B. This is Gause's competitive exclusion principle: two species with identical requirements cannot coexist indefinitely on one limiting resource, and the better competitor displaces the other. In the field, coexistence happens through niche differentiation — different depths, times or food sizes.

  4. Nucleolus

In human blood, oxygen is transported chiefly by

  1. plasma proteins
  2. haemoglobin in red blood cells
  3. white blood cells
  4. platelets

Answer: B. Haemoglobin binds oxygen reversibly at its iron centres; only a small fraction dissolves in plasma. Carbon monoxide's danger lies in binding the same site with far greater affinity.

  3. Nitrobacter
  4. Azotobacter

  1. alveolus

  3. neuron
  4. villus

Photosynthesis in green plants converts light energy into

  1. heat energy
  2. chemical energy
  3. mechanical energy
  4. electrical energy

Answer: B. Light drives the synthesis of carbohydrate, storing energy in chemical bonds. The light reactions do make ATP and NADPH first, but the stored form is chemical throughout.

Which one of the following blood groups is called the universal donor?

  1. AB positive
  2. A negative
  3. O negative
  4. B positive

Answer: C. O negative red cells carry neither A nor B antigen nor the Rh D antigen, so they provoke no reaction in any recipient. AB positive is the universal recipient — the reciprocal fact, and the intended trap.

The hormone insulin is secreted by

  2. beta cells of the pancreas
  3. the adrenal cortex
  4. the pituitary gland

Answer: B. Beta cells of the islets of Langerhans secrete insulin, which lowers blood glucose; alpha cells secrete glucagon, which raises it.

  4. Spleen

A bed strikes N30°E and dips 70° towards SE. On a vertical E–W road section the bed will appear to dip at approximately

  1. 70°
  2. 67°
  3. 45°
  4. 35°

Answer: B. tan δ = tan φ × sin θ, where θ is the angle between the section and the strike. Here θ = 60°, so tan δ = tan 70° × sin 60° = 2.747 × 0.866 = 2.379 and δ = 67°. The apparent dip is always less than the true dip, and only slightly less when the section is nearly perpendicular to strike.

50 marks10 × 5

Answer the following in about 150 words each, with sketches where appropriate:

  1. Hjulström diagram and its significance. 5

    Velocity against grain size, with an entrainment curve and a deposition curve. The entrainment curve has a minimum at fine sand (about 0.2 mm) and turns upward for silt and clay because cohesion must be overcome; the deposition curve falls monotonically. Consequences: fine sand is the most easily moved sediment; clay, once suspended, travels far; and a river can transport what it cannot re-entrain.

  2. Upper flow-regime bedforms. 5

    As Froude number approaches unity, dunes wash out to a plane bed with parting lineation; above unity, in-phase antidunes form and migrate upstream. Distinguish from lower-regime ripples and dunes, which are out of phase with the water surface. Preserved plane-bed lamination and antidune cross-sets indicate high-energy shallow flow.

  3. Rayleigh and Mie scattering. 5

    Rayleigh scattering acts where particles are much smaller than the wavelength and varies as λ−4, so it dominates the blue end — hence blue sky and hazy blue bands in satellite imagery. Mie scattering acts where particle size is comparable to the wavelength (aerosols, dust, water droplets), is far less wavelength-selective, and produces white haze. Non-selective scattering by still larger particles gives white cloud.

  4. Paracrystalline deformation. 5

    Deformation contemporaneous with mineral growth, recorded by textures that neither pre- nor post-date the porphyroblast: helicitic inclusion trails continuous with the external foliation, snowball garnet with spiralled inclusions, and syn-tectonic strain shadows. Its value is in dating deformation relative to metamorphism.

  5. Wave-ravinement surface. 5

    An erosional surface cut by the migrating shoreface during transgression, commonly overlain by a thin coarse lag and shallow-marine sand and underlain by non-marine or lagoonal deposits. It is a transgressive surface, distinct from a sequence boundary formed during relative fall, and it commonly removes the record of the transgression itself.

  6. Truncated spurs and hanging valleys. 5

    Both record glacial over-deepening of a trunk valley. A trunk glacier cuts off the ends of interlocking spurs, leaving triangular truncated facets; tributary glaciers erode less deeply, so their floors are left above the trunk floor and their valleys hang, commonly with a waterfall at the lip. Both are diagnostic of glaciated as against fluvial valleys.

  7. Derived fossils. 5

    Fossils reworked from an older bed into a younger one, so their age is older than the containing sediment. Recognised by abrasion, staining and discordance with the rest of the assemblage. They matter because a single derived specimen can misdate a horizon; correlation should rest on the youngest consistent assemblage.

  8. Concurrent range zone. 5

    The interval in which the ranges of two or more named taxa overlap; the most precise ordinary biozone because it is bounded by two independent datums. Contrast with the assemblage zone (whole fauna), acme zone (peak abundance) and interval zone (between successive datums).

  9. Purana basins. 5

    Proterozoic intracratonic basins of peninsular India — Cuddapah, Vindhyan, Chhattisgarh, Kaladgi, Bhima, Pranhita–Godavari — filled with thick, flat-lying, essentially unmetamorphosed sandstone, shale and limestone with stromatolites and minor volcanics, resting unconformably on Archaean basement. They record long-lived intracratonic subsidence rather than orogeny.

  10. Vertical exaggeration in aerial photographs. 5

    Under a stereoscope relief appears steeper than it is, because the base-to-height ratio of the photographs exceeds the ratio of the observer's eye base to viewing distance. VE ≈ (air base / flying height) ÷ (eye base / viewing distance), typically 2–4. It aids detection of subtle relief but leads to overestimation of slope angles and dips.

Balancing eliminates impossible interpretations rather than proving the correct one.

Q5

30 marksStructural geology
  1. Explain the Coulomb–Mohr failure criterion and use it to account for the orientation of conjugate shear fractures and for the three Andersonian fault regimes. 15

    τ = c + σn tan φ, a straight envelope on a Mohr diagram; failure occurs where the stress circle touches it. The tangent point gives 2θ = 90° + φ, so each shear plane lies at 45° − φ/2 to σ1 and the conjugate pair encloses an acute angle bisected by σ1. Since the free surface carries no shear, one principal stress must be vertical: σ1 vertical gives normal faults dipping about 60°, σ2 vertical gives vertical strike-slip faults, σ3 vertical gives thrusts dipping about 30°.

  2. Distinguish pure shear from simple shear, and explain how shear sense is determined in a mylonite. 15

    Pure shear is coaxial and irrotational: the strain axes stay on the same material lines. Simple shear is non-coaxial: the axes rotate towards the shear plane, which is what makes asymmetry possible at all. Indicators: S–C fabrics, asymmetric porphyroclast tails (σ and δ types), mica fish, oblique grain-shape foliation, quartz c-axis fabric asymmetry, displaced markers, drag folds. Soundness comes from agreement among several independent indicators on a section cut parallel to the lineation and perpendicular to the foliation.

Section A (compulsory)

Q1

50 marks10 × 5

Answer the following in about 150 words each, with sketches where appropriate:

  1. Schottky and Frenkel defects. 5

    Both are point defects. A Schottky defect is a paired cation and anion vacancy, so charge balance is kept and density falls. A Frenkel defect displaces an ion to an interstitial site, leaving a vacancy behind, with no density change. Defects control diffusion, ionic conductivity and colour centres, and their concentration rises with temperature.

  2. Interference figures and their use. 5

    Obtained in convergent light with a high-power objective and the Bertrand lens inserted. A uniaxial figure shows a black cross whose isogyres stay together on rotation; a biaxial figure shows two isogyres that separate, and their curvature estimates 2V. With an accessory plate the figure gives optic sign: for a uniaxial positive mineral the retardation rises in the NE and SW quadrants. It is the fastest route from a grain to a mineral name.

  3. Pyroxene quadrilateral. 5

    The Ca–Mg–Fe compositional field bounded by enstatite–ferrosilite below and diopside–hedenbergite above, on which pyroxene compositions are plotted. The wide two-pyroxene gap between orthopyroxene and augite narrows with rising temperature, so coexisting compositions give a thermometer, and exsolution lamellae record cooling.

  4. Fick's laws of diffusion. 5

    First law: flux is proportional to the concentration gradient, J = −D ∂C/∂x, for steady state. Second law: ∂C/∂t = D ∂²C/∂x², for the transient case. D rises exponentially with temperature by an Arrhenius law. Geological use: zoning profiles in garnet and plagioclase are diffusion clocks, giving the duration of heating and cooling rates.

  5. Binary eutectic and peritectic. 5

    At a eutectic, liquid crystallises two solids simultaneously at the lowest temperature of the system, as in diopside–anorthite. At a peritectic, liquid reacts with an existing solid to yield a new solid, as in forsterite + liquid → enstatite in the forsterite–silica system; the earlier phase may be resorbed, which is why reaction rims and reversely zoned crystals occur.

  6. Total alkali–silica diagram. 5

    The IUGS TAS classification of volcanic rocks: Na2O + K2O against SiO2 in weight per cent, divided into fields from picrobasalt through basalt, andesite and dacite to rhyolite, with alkaline fields above the Irvine–Baragar line. It is used where the rock is too fine-grained for a modal name, on analyses recalculated volatile-free to 100%.

  7. Geothermobarometry. 5

    Estimating P and T from the composition of coexisting minerals. A thermometer uses a strongly temperature-dependent exchange reaction with small volume change, such as garnet–biotite Fe–Mg exchange; a barometer uses a reaction with a large volume change, such as garnet–aluminosilicate–plagioclase–quartz. Intersecting the two curves gives peak conditions, provided the pair is demonstrably in equilibrium and has not been reset by diffusion.

  8. Curie point and blocking temperature. 5

    The Curie point is where spontaneous magnetisation disappears — 580°C for magnetite, 680°C for haematite. The blocking temperature, reached lower on cooling, is where relaxation time becomes long enough that a grain's remanence is locked in. The distinction matters because a rock's palaeomagnetic record dates from its blocking temperature, not its formation.

  9. Ring of Fire. 5

    The horseshoe of subduction-related volcanism and seismicity around the Pacific, from the Andes through the Aleutians to Japan, the Philippines and New Zealand. It carries about 75% of the world's active volcanoes and most large earthquakes, and its andesitic to dacitic, water-rich, explosive volcanism follows from melting a hydrated slab — a direct consequence of plate convergence.

  10. Continent–continent collision. 5

    Closure of an intervening ocean brings two buoyant continents together: subduction stalls, the crust thickens by thrust stacking and folding, a suture with ophiolite slivers marks the join, Barrovian metamorphism and crustal melting follow, and a foreland basin fills with molasse. The Himalaya, with the Indus–Tsangpo suture and the Siwalik foreland, is the type example.

  1. Explain symmetry elements, point groups and space groups, and how translational symmetry elements are recognised in X-ray data. 15

    Point symmetry: rotation axes 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, mirror planes, a centre, and roto-inversion axes; their compatible combinations give the 32 crystal classes, which distribute among the seven systems.   2. Describe the twin laws of feldspar and how they are identified under the microscope. 15

    Albite law: repeated lamellar twinning on {010}, giving plagioclase's fine polysynthetic striping. Pericline law: twinning about the b axis, which combines with albite twinning in microcline to give the cross-hatched tartan. Carlsbad: penetration twinning about the c axis, common in orthoclase. Manebach on {001} and Baveno on {021}, both contact twins. Identification: crossed nicols with the plane of the twin composition perpendicular to the section; the two sets extinguish at equal but opposite angles to the trace, and Michel-Lévy's method uses that maximum extinction angle to estimate An content.

30 marksMineralogy
  1. Classify the silicates by the polymerisation of the SiO4 tetrahedra, giving the Si:O ratio and an example of each, and explain how structure controls cleavage and hardness. 15

    Neso 1:4 olivine; soro 2:7 epidote; cyclo 1:3 beryl and tourmaline; ino single chain 1:3 pyroxene, double chain 4:11 amphibole; phyllo 2:5 mica and clay; tecto 1:2 quartz and feldspar. Structural control: isolated tetrahedra give strong bonding in all directions, hence no good cleavage and high hardness in olivine and garnet; chains give two cleavages, at nearly 90° in pyroxene and 56°/124° in amphibole, because the cleavage passes between chains; sheets give one perfect basal cleavage; frameworks give no cleavage but high hardness. Substitution of Al for Si in tetrahedral sites requires interlayer cations for charge balance, which is why micas are soft where quartz is hard.

  2. Explain the optical properties used to identify minerals in thin section, and how each is measured. 15

    In plane-polarised light: relief and refractive index by the Becke line and immersion, colour and pleochroism on rotation, habit and cleavage. Under crossed nicols: birefringence from the highest interference colour, corrected for section thickness on the Michel-Lévy chart; extinction angle relative to a cleavage or twin trace; twinning; and zoning. In convergent light: uniaxial or biaxial character, 2V and optic sign from the interference figure with an accessory plate. Practical order — work from relief and cleavage to colour, then birefringence, then extinction angle, then the interference figure, since each step narrows the field.

30 marksGeochemistry
  1. Explain the distribution of trace elements between crystals and melt, and how REE patterns are used to infer petrogenesis. 15

    Partitioning is quantified by the Nernst coefficient KD and, for a rock, the bulk D. Elements with D < 1 are incompatible and concentrate in the melt; D > 1 compatible and concentrate in the crystals. Batch melting gives CL/C0 = 1/[D(1−F)+F], Rayleigh fractionation CL/C0 = F(D−1). REE patterns, chondrite-normalised: LREE enrichment indicates small melt fractions or an enriched source; a negative Eu anomaly plagioclase removal; heavy-REE depletion garnet in the residue, hence deep melting; and a flat pattern near unity a primitive source. The reasoning must run from the shape of the pattern to the mineral responsible.

  2. Discuss the Rb–Sr and Sm–Nd systems and what an isochron and an initial ratio each tell you. 15

    Both are decay schemes plotted as daughter/stable against parent/stable, so a suite of cogenetic samples with the same initial ratio defines a line whose slope is eλt−1 and whose intercept is the initial ratio. Rb–Sr: λ small, easily disturbed by alteration and metamorphism because Rb and Sr are mobile, so it dates the last equilibration as often as the formation. Sm–Nd: REE are far less mobile, so it survives metamorphism better; the initial ratio expressed as εNd distinguishes depleted-mantle from crustal sources, and model ages give crustal residence time. The examinable contrast is robustness against source information.

Q5

30 marksGeochemistry
  1. Explain the phase rule and its application to metamorphic and igneous assemblages. 15

    F = C − P + 2 for a system whose only intensive variables are P and T. Goldschmidt's mineralogical phase rule notes that a rock which equilibrated over a range of P and T should be divariant, so P ≤ C; more phases than components implies a univariant or invariant condition — an isograd or an invariant point. In igneous systems the rule explains why a binary eutectic is invariant at fixed pressure, and why a ternary liquid follows a cotectic line before reaching a eutectic point. Careful choice of components, and stating whether fluid is present, is where marks are won or lost.

  2. Discuss Eh–pH diagrams and their use in interpreting weathering and ore deposition. 15

    Axes are redox potential and pH; the field is bounded above and below by the stability of water. Boundaries between mineral fields are calculated from the Nernst equation at a stated activity. Uses: iron speciation, with Fe2+ soluble in reducing acid water and haematite or goethite precipitating on oxidation, which explains gossans, laterite and banded iron formation; uranium mobile as uranyl in oxidising water and fixed at a redox front, which is the roll-front deposit; and sulphide stability against sulphate, which governs acid mine drainage. Note that the diagrams assume equilibrium and stated activities, so they bound possibilities rather than predict rates.

30 marksIgneous petrology
  1. Explain, with phase diagrams, how a basaltic magma can produce a differentiated suite, and how the products of fractionation are distinguished from those of partial melting. 15

    Mechanisms: fractional crystallisation with crystal settling or flow segregation, magma mixing, assimilation with fractional crystallisation, and liquid immiscibility. Draw the diopside–anorthite binary for a simple eutectic, forsterite–silica for a peritectic, and albite–anorthite for a solid solution with continuous zoning. Distinguishing fractionation from melting: fractionation drives correlated changes in compatible and incompatible elements along a liquid line of descent, produces cumulate rocks as complements, and preserves phenocryst zoning; partial melting varies incompatible ratios strongly at near-constant compatible content, and leaves a residue rather than a cumulate. Isotopes are decisive — closed-system fractionation cannot change 87Sr/86Sr, whereas assimilation does.

  2. Describe the S-, I- and A-type granite classification, with the criteria and the tectonic setting of each. 15

    S-type: melting of metasedimentary protolith; peraluminous, ASI > 1.1, muscovite and cordierite, normative corundum, high δ18O and initial 87Sr/86Sr, ilmenite series; syn-collisional. I-type: melting of igneous protolith; metaluminous, hornblende and magnetite, lower initial Sr; continental arc. A-type: anorogenic; alkaline to peralkaline, high Fe/Mg, high Zr, Nb, Ga, Y and F, commonly rapakivi-textured; rift and post-orogenic settings. M-type from mantle-derived melt completes the scheme. Marks follow from tying the mineralogical criterion to the protolith and setting, not from the labels.

Q7

30 marksMetamorphic petrology
  1. Describe the metamorphic facies and facies series, and explain what a facies series indicates about tectonic setting. 15

    Facies: zeolite, prehnite–pumpellyite, greenschist, blueschist, amphibolite, granulite, eclogite, and the three hornfels facies of contact metamorphism — each a P–T field recognised from a diagnostic assemblage in a basic rock. Facies series link the facies a terrain passes through, hence its geothermal gradient: low-P/high-T Buchan or Abukuma with andalusite and cordierite implies a hot, thin crust or a magmatic arc; medium-P Barrovian with kyanite implies normal collisional thickening; high-P/low-T Sanbagawa with blueschist and eclogite implies subduction. Paired belts pin arc against trench in one orogen.

  2. Discuss the textures of metamorphic rocks and what they reveal about the relative timing of deformation and crystallisation. 15

    Textures: granoblastic, porphyroblastic, poikiloblastic, nematoblastic, lepidoblastic, decussate, and the shear-zone series mylonite to phyllonite. Timing is read from porphyroblast–matrix relations: pre-tectonic porphyroblasts have inclusion trails discordant with the external foliation and are wrapped by it; syn-tectonic ones show helicitic or spiralled trails continuous with it; post-tectonic ones overgrow the foliation without deflecting it. Add pressure shadows, strain caps, and the crenulation-cleavage sequence, and a single thin section yields a relative chronology of deformation and metamorphism.

Q8

30 marksMetamorphic petrology
  1. Discuss migmatites: their nomenclature, mechanisms of formation and criteria for recognising partial melting in the field. 15

    Nomenclature: leucosome (crystallised melt), melanosome (residue), mesosome or palaeosome (unaffected host); morphologies include stromatic, schlieren, nebulitic, diktyonitic and agmatitic. Mechanisms: partial melting in situ at temperatures above the wet or dehydration solidus; melt injection from elsewhere; and metamorphic differentiation or subsolidus segregation, which is not melting at all. Field criteria for true melting: peritectic minerals such as garnet or cordierite in the leucosome, cuspate grain boundaries against quartz, dilational structures, leucosome discordant to the foliation, and a K-feldspar-rich composition close to the minimum melt. Absence of these points to segregation rather than melting.

  2. Explain how a P–T–t path is reconstructed and what a clockwise path implies. 15

    Reconstruction: successive assemblages and their thermobarometry; garnet zoning as a growth record; inclusion suites in porphyroblasts giving earlier stages; reaction textures such as kyanite–sillimanite transitions or cordierite coronas on garnet giving the later ones; and geochronology on monazite or zircon to attach times. A clockwise path — burial and heating to peak pressure, then heating to peak temperature, then near-isothermal decompression — is characteristic of collisional thickening followed by rapid exhumation, so the peaks of P and T are not simultaneous. An anticlockwise path, with heating before burial, points to magmatic loading or arc settings.

30 marksIgneous petrology
  1. Describe the ophiolite sequence and its significance. 15

    From base up: tectonised mantle harzburgite; cumulate ultramafics and layered gabbro; isotropic gabbro; a sheeted dyke complex; pillow basalt; and pelagic sediment with chert and limestone. Significance: it is a section of oceanic lithosphere obducted onto continental crust, so its presence marks a suture and a closed ocean; the sheeted dykes prove spreading; the mantle–crust transition locates a palaeo-Moho. Indian examples: the Indus–Tsangpo suture ophiolites, Naga Hills and Andaman. Podiform chromite and Cyprus-type sulphides give the economic interest.

  2. Discuss the origin and significance of carbonatites and their Indian occurrences. 15

    Carbonatites are igneous rocks with more than 50% modal carbonate, of calcite, dolomite or ferrocarbonatite type. Origins: liquid immiscibility from a CO2-rich alkaline silicate magma, extreme fractionation of a nephelinitic melt, or direct low-degree melting of carbonated mantle. Setting: continental rifts and alkaline complexes, commonly with fenitised aureoles. Significance: the world's main source of REE and niobium, plus phosphate and fluorite, and a probe of mantle volatiles. Indian occurrences: Amba Dongar, Sung Valley, Newania, Sevathur and Samalpatti — several tied to rift-related magmatism.

30 marksGeodynamics
  1. Describe the internal structure of the Earth and the seismological evidence for each major boundary. 15

    Layers and boundaries: crust to mantle at the Moho, from the P-velocity jump 6–7 to 8 km/s; the low-velocity zone at 100–200 km, from delayed and attenuated S arrivals; transition-zone discontinuities at 410 and 660 km, from triplications in travel-time curves; mantle to outer core at the Gutenberg discontinuity at 2891 km, from the P shadow beyond 103° and the total absence of direct S; and the Lehmann discontinuity at 5150 km, from PKiKP reflections and inner-core shear-wave evidence.   2. Explain the evidence for sea-floor spreading and how magnetic anomalies are used to date the ocean floor. 15

    Evidence: symmetrical magnetic stripes about ridge axes; increasing sediment thickness and basement age away from the ridge, confirmed by drilling; heat flow highest at the axis; shallow ridge-crest seismicity with transform-fault mechanisms; and the age of the oldest ocean floor, about 180 Ma, far younger than continents. Dating: the Vine–Matthews hypothesis reads the stripes as normal and reversed magnetisation frozen in cooling basalt, so a stripe pattern matched against the calibrated reversal timescale gives crustal age, and the spacing gives spreading rate.

Q11

30 marksGeodynamics
  1. Discuss isostasy, the Airy and Pratt models, and the significance of gravity anomalies over mountain belts and ocean trenches. 15

    Isostasy is flotation equilibrium of the lithosphere on the asthenosphere. Airy: constant density with varying thickness, so mountains have roots — a root of about 5 km for each 1 km of elevation from the density contrast. Pratt: constant depth of compensation with varying density. Vening Meinesz adds regional flexural support. Gravity: the free-air anomaly is near zero over compensated mountains, while a large negative Bouguer anomaly reveals the low-density root; trenches show strong negative free-air anomalies because subduction holds the plate down out of equilibrium; mid-ocean ridges are broadly compensated. The interpretive point is that the free-air anomaly tests compensation and the Bouguer anomaly reveals structure.

  2. Explain the origin of the geomagnetic field and the record of reversals. 15

    Origin: a self-exciting dynamo in the convecting, electrically conducting liquid outer core, driven by thermal and compositional buoyancy and organised by rotation; about 90% of the surface field is dipolar, with the non-dipole part drifting westward as secular variation. Reversals: recorded as normal and reversed remanence in cooling basalt and in sediments, they occur irregularly at 105–106-year intervals with long superchrons between; a reversal takes a few thousand years, during which the dipole weakens. Uses: magnetostratigraphy, sea-floor dating, and the present decline of dipole strength as evidence of ongoing core dynamics.

Q1

50 marks10 × 5

Answer the following in about 150 words each, with sketches where appropriate:

  1. Metallogenic epochs and provinces. 5

    An epoch is an interval of geological time favourable to a kind of mineralisation — banded iron formations in the Palaeoproterozoic, porphyry copper mostly Mesozoic–Cenozoic. A province is a region characterised by an ore association, such as the Aravalli Pb–Zn belt or the Singhbhum copper belt. Epoch is time, province is space; together they express the tectonic and atmospheric controls on ore formation.

  2. Nugget effect. 5

    The non-zero intercept of a variogram at zero separation, arising from variability at scales below the sampling interval plus analytical and sampling error. A high nugget-to-sill ratio means adjacent samples are nearly uncorrelated, so estimation variance stays high whatever the drilling density — which is why coarse gold deposits demand far more sampling than iron ore for the same confidence.

  3. Pathfinder elements. 5

    Elements more easily detected or more mobile than the target metal but consistently associated with it, so their anomaly points to ore: As, Sb and Hg for gold; Mo for porphyry copper; He and Rn for uranium; Zn for concealed Pb–Zn. They enlarge the target and can survive where the ore metal itself has been leached.

  4. Vitrinite reflectance. 5

    The percentage of incident light reflected by vitrinite under oil immersion, Ro, measured on polished blocks. It rises irreversibly with maximum palaeotemperature, so it is the standard rank and maturity index: about 0.5% at the top of the oil window, 1.3% at the bottom, above 2% dry gas only. Because it does not reset on cooling it records burial history rather than present temperature.

  5. Coal bed methane. 5

    Methane generated during coalification and held adsorbed on the internal surfaces of coal, so gas content rises with rank and with pressure. Production requires dewatering to lower reservoir pressure and desorb the gas, with cleat permeability controlling flow. Indian prospects lie in the Damodar valley — Jharia, Raniganj, Bokaro — and the Cambay basin.

  6. Rock Quality Designation. 5

    RQD is the summed length of intact core pieces longer than 10 cm expressed as a percentage of the run, measured on NX or larger core. Deere's scale runs very poor to excellent at 0–25–50–75–90–100. It is a measure of fracture spacing, not of intact strength, and it is directional; it enters the RMR and Q rock-mass classifications used to choose tunnel support.

  7. Reservoir-triggered seismicity. 5

    Earthquakes induced by impounding a reservoir, through the added load and, more importantly, increased pore pressure reducing effective normal stress on pre-existing faults. It needs a critically stressed fault and a hydraulic path, so it is most likely in the first years of filling and during rapid drawdown. Koyna is the type Indian example, with continuing activity since 1967.

  8. Sodium adsorption ratio. 5

    SAR = Na+ / √[(Ca2+ + Mg2+)/2], in meq/l. It measures the sodium hazard of irrigation water: high SAR promotes exchange of Na for Ca on clays, which disperses aggregates and destroys soil structure and permeability. Read with electrical conductivity on the US Salinity Laboratory diagram to classify water for irrigation.

  9. Acid mine drainage. 5

    Oxidation of pyrite exposed in workings, waste and tailings produces sulphuric acid and ferrous iron; further oxidation and hydrolysis generate more acidity and ochre precipitate, and bacteria such as Acidithiobacillus accelerate the reaction by orders of magnitude. The low pH mobilises heavy metals. Control: prevent contact with air and water, add alkaline material, treat with wetlands or limestone drains, and reclaim promptly.

  10. Carbon capture and storage. 5

    Capture from point sources, compression, and injection into a reservoir at more than about 800 m so CO2 remains supercritical and dense. Suitable formations: deep saline aquifers with a competent seal, depleted oil and gas fields (with enhanced recovery as a by-product), and unmineable coal seams. Trapping is structural, then residual, then by dissolution and mineralisation. Indian options include Deccan basalt, where CO2 mineralises rapidly, and the Cambay and Krishna–Godavari basins.

30 marksMineral exploration
  1. Outline the stages of a mineral exploration programme and the methods appropriate to each. 15

    Reconnaissance: target selection from regional geology, aeromagnetic and satellite data, stream-sediment geochemistry — large areas, cheap methods. Prospecting: geological mapping, pitting and trenching, soil geochemistry, ground geophysics on selected anomalies. General exploration: wide-spaced drilling to establish continuity and give an inferred resource. Detailed exploration: close-spaced drilling, bulk sampling, metallurgical testing, to indicated and measured categories. Feasibility: mine design, economics, environmental clearance. The governing principle is a funnel — expenditure per unit area rises as area falls, and each stage must justify the next.

  2. Compare geochemical and geophysical methods of exploration, and state what each detects. 15

    Geochemical methods detect chemical dispersion of ore-related elements: stream sediment for regional targeting, soil and lithogeochemistry for local, biogeochemistry and vapour surveys in special cases. They respond directly to the metals sought but only to what has dispersed, so cover thickness limits them. Geophysical methods detect physical contrasts: magnetics for magnetite and mafic bodies; gravity for dense sulphide masses; resistivity and EM for conductive massive sulphide; IP for disseminated sulphide; radiometrics for U, Th and K; seismic for structure. They see through cover but respond to properties, not to ore, so a conductor may be graphite. Best practice combines them and drills only coincident anomalies.

  1. Discuss ore reserve estimation, from sampling to a classified resource. 15

    Sampling: channel, chip, core and bulk samples, reduced by coning and quartering, with duplicates and standards for quality control. Q6

30 marksFuel geology
  1. Describe the origin, migration and entrapment of petroleum, and the elements a prospect must have. 15

    Origin: marine or lacustrine organic matter preserved under anoxia, converted through diagenesis to kerogen and then, in the oil window at roughly 60–120°C or Ro 0.5–1.3%, to oil, with gas above. Primary migration expels hydrocarbons from the compacting source rock; secondary migration is buoyancy-driven movement through carrier beds to a trap; tertiary migration is leakage to surface. Entrapment needs a reservoir of adequate porosity and permeability, a seal, and a closed geometry — structural, stratigraphic or combination. A prospect therefore requires source, maturity, migration path, reservoir, seal, trap and correct timing; failure of any one is a dry hole, and timing is the element most often missed.

  2. Discuss Indian coal resources and the differences between Gondwana and Tertiary coals. 15

    Gondwana coals, about 98% of reserves, occur in Permian rift basins of the Damodar, Son–Mahanadi, Pranhita–Godavari and Satpura valleys — Jharia, Raniganj, Bokaro, Korba, Singrauli, Talcher. They are bituminous, low in sulphur, high in ash, with Jharia the only substantial prime coking coal. Tertiary coals of the north-east (Makum, Meghalaya) and Rajasthan and Gujarat lignites are younger, low in ash but high in sulphur, and lignites are used mainly for power at Neyveli and Kachchh. The examinable contrast is the ash and sulphur inversion, which follows from depositional setting: freshwater Gondwana swamps with detrital ash against brackish Tertiary swamps with marine sulphate.

30 marksEngineering geology
  1. Discuss the geological investigations required for a dam site and the problems each is meant to anticipate. 15

    Investigations: regional geology and seismotectonics; mapping of lithology, weathering profile and discontinuities at abutments and foundation; drilling with core recovery, RQD and permeability (Lugeon) tests; geophysics for bedrock depth and weathered-zone thickness; laboratory strength and deformability tests; and reservoir-rim examination.     RMR sums ratings for intact strength, RQD, discontinuity spacing, discontinuity condition and groundwater, with an adjustment for orientation relative to the drive, and returns five classes with stand-up time and support recommendations. The Q system multiplies and divides six parameters — RQD/Jn, Jr/Ja, Jw/SRF — representing block size, shear strength and active stress, and plots against equivalent dimension to select bolting and shotcrete. GSI feeds Hoek–Brown strength estimation for numerical modelling. Limits: they are empirical and derived from case histories, so they must be checked against squeezing-ground and rock-burst criteria in high-stress Himalayan tunnels.

Q8

30 marksHydrogeology
  1. Derive or state the Theis and Cooper–Jacob solutions and explain how a pumping test yields transmissivity and storativity. 15

    Theis: s = (Q/4πT) W(u), with u = r²S/4Tt, assuming a homogeneous, isotropic, confined aquifer of infinite extent, fully penetrating well, constant discharge and instantaneous release from storage. Matching field drawdown against the type curve gives T and S. Cooper–Jacob approximates W(u) for small u by a logarithm: s = (2.303Q/4πT) log(2.25Tt/r²S), so drawdown against log time is a straight line, T = 2.303Q/4πΔs per log cycle and S from the zero-drawdown intercept. Departures from the straight line are themselves informative — leakage flattens it, a barrier boundary steepens it, delayed yield gives the S-shaped unconfined curve that Boulton and Neuman treat.

  2. Discuss sea-water intrusion in a coastal aquifer, its recognition and its control. 15

    Mechanism: fresh water floats on denser saline water, with the interface at about 40 times the freshwater head below sea level by the Ghyben–Herzberg relation, so a 1 m drop in water table raises the interface by roughly 40 m. Over-pumping causes landward migration of the wedge and upconing beneath wells. Recognition: rising chloride and EC, Cl/Br ratios, a Na–Cl hydrochemical facies on a Piper diagram, reversed hydraulic gradients, and resistivity surveys or monitoring boreholes imaging the interface. Control: reduce and redistribute pumping, shift abstraction inland, keep wells shallow to avoid upconing, artificial recharge and freshwater injection barriers, extraction barriers, subsurface cut-offs, and tidal regulators — with monitoring, since the wedge responds slowly.

30 marksHydrogeology
  1. Describe the groundwater provinces of India and the problems characteristic of each. 15

    Indo-Gangetic and coastal alluvium: thick, highly productive multi-layer aquifers; problems are declining water tables in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, waterlogging and salinity in canal commands, and arsenic in Holocene sediments of the Bengal basin. Hard-rock peninsular India: limited storage in the weathered mantle and fractures, low yields, rapid seasonal decline, and fluoride problems in granitic terrain. Himalayan and sub-Himalayan: spring-fed, structurally controlled, with the Bhabar and Terai belts important. Desert and arid west: deep water tables and high salinity. Karst: high but erratic yields with severe contamination risk. Management must therefore differ by province, which is the point of the classification.

  2. Explain artificial recharge and the criteria for selecting sites and structures. 15

    Purpose: store monsoon surplus underground where evaporation is negligible, arrest declining water tables, improve quality by dilution, and resist sea-water intrusion. Structures: percolation tanks, check dams and nala bunds, recharge shafts and trenches, injection wells in confined aquifers, spreading basins, and rooftop rainwater harvesting. Site criteria: a demonstrated unsaturated zone with storage available; adequate vertical permeability from surface to aquifer, so avoid thick clay; source water of adequate quantity and acceptable quality with silt control; a favourable slope and catchment; and no risk of waterlogging. Design also needs desilting arrangements and post-project monitoring of water levels and quality; recharge schemes fail most often through clogging.

30 marksEnvironmental geology
  1. Discuss landslide hazard in the Himalaya: causes, and the elements of a hazard zonation map. 15

    Causes, separated into preparatory and triggering. Preparatory: young, weak and highly fractured rock; steep slopes; adverse discontinuity orientation; thick colluvium; high relief and active uplift. Triggering: intense monsoon and cloudburst rainfall raising pore pressure; earthquakes; undercutting by rivers; and human action — road cutting without support, deforestation, unplanned construction, uncontrolled drainage. Zonation: overlay of slope, aspect, lithology, structure, land use, drainage proximity and rainfall, each weighted, to give a landslide susceptibility index in classes from very low to very high, validated against an inventory of past slides. Mitigation follows the map: benching, retaining structures, subsurface drainage, bioengineering and, where necessary, realignment.

  2. Explain arsenic and fluoride contamination of groundwater in India, their mechanisms and their remedies. 15

    Arsenic: geogenic, in the Holocene alluvium of the Bengal basin and the middle Ganga plain. Reductive dissolution of Fe-oxyhydroxides, driven by buried organic matter, releases sorbed As into shallow reducing groundwater; the deeper Pleistocene aquifer is generally safe. Remedies: deeper or alternative sources, surface water, oxidation and coprecipitation, adsorption on activated alumina or iron media, and blanket testing, since concentration varies well to well. Fluoride: leaching of fluorite, apatite and micas in granitic and gneissic terrain of Rajasthan, Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, worst where evaporation concentrates alkaline water. Remedies: Nalgonda defluoridation, activated alumina, dilution by recharge and rainwater harvesting, and nutritional support. Both need mapping at village scale, because the hazard is spatially erratic.

30 marksEnvironmental geology
  1. Discuss the environmental impact of mining and the components of a mine closure plan. 15

    Impacts: land degradation and loss of forest and topsoil; overburden dumps and subsidence over underground workings; acid mine drainage and heavy-metal release; siltation of streams; groundwater depletion from dewatering and disruption of aquifers; dust and blasting noise; tailings-dam risk; and social displacement. Closure plan: progressive backfilling and regrading to a stable landform; topsoil replacement and revegetation with native species; safe closure of shafts and pit slopes; long-term water treatment where AMD is a risk; tailings capping and monitoring; conversion of voids to reservoirs where safe; and a financial guarantee for post-closure monitoring. Statutory framework: EIA under the EIA Notification, forest and wildlife clearance, and the progressive and final mine closure plans required under the MMDR framework.

  2. Explain seismic zonation of India and its use in planning. 15

    IS 1893 divides the country into four zones, II to V, by expected intensity, with zone factors 0.10, 0.16, 0.24 and 0.36. Zone V covers the Kachchh, the Andaman–Nicobar islands, the north-east and parts of the Himalaya and the Indo-Gangetic plain near the foothills; zone II the stable interior of the peninsula. Basis: past seismicity and intensity records, tectonic setting and active faults. Uses: design of buildings, dams, bridges and lifelines; land-use planning; retrofitting priority. Limitations: the map is intensity-based, does not treat site amplification or liquefaction, and the peninsular shield has produced damaging events at Koyna, Latur and Jabalpur — hence the case for probabilistic hazard assessment and site-specific microzonation for critical structures.

7. How to use these papers

INFERENCE Sit the two prelims papers first, under their own conditions: 120 items in two hours each, no notes, and mark only what you would actually mark in the hall. Then score each twice — once counting every attempt, once applying the penalty. The gap between the two scores is the cost of your guessing, and it is usually the largest single controllable loss on either paper.

The arithmetic worth internalising, for Paper II: a blind guess on a four-option item is worth (0.25 × 2.5) − (0.75 × 0.83) = −0.42 marks. Eliminate one option and it is (0.33 × 2.5) − (0.67 × 0.83) = +0.28. Eliminate two and it is +1.25 − 0.42 = +0.83. Paper I has the same structure at a third the scale: −0.14 blind, +0.09 with one eliminated, +0.28 with two. So the rule is not ‘never guess’ but ‘never guess blind’, and the skill to practise is elimination rather than recall.

Sequence the two papers as the exam does, Paper I in the morning and Paper II in the afternoon, at least once. Paper I is the qualifying gate and the one candidates neglect: 100 marks against Paper II's 300, none of them counted in the final ranking, and yet a cut-off missed there ends the attempt with three prepared mains papers unwritten. Note also where the syllabi overlap — glacial landforms, water hardness, isotopes, coral reefs, monsoon and salinity all appear in both above — because that overlap is the cheapest preparation available anywhere in this exam.

For the mains papers, do not write them out in full at first. Take Q1 of each — the ten short notes — and write those to time: 50 marks in about 45 minutes, which is four or five sentences and a labelled sketch each. That block is a third of the printed marks in every mains paper (34% of all parts across 2014–2026), it is compulsory, and it is where preparation converts most directly into marks. Then attempt one 30-mark question from each section, choosing as you would in the hall, and compare your outline against the model answers rather than your prose.

SPECULATIVE What these papers can and cannot do. They can rehearse the concepts the examiner has returned to most often, in the moulds and at the marks pressure the real papers use. They cannot tell you which questions will be set, and the topic allocation carries the full uncertainty documented in report 5 — a projected figure is the centre of a range about half as wide again. Three further limits are specific to these pages: no official answer keys exist in this collection, so the answers here are the compiler's; the item moulds are measurably heavier on plain recall than the real papers, in both prelims papers; and the General Studies allocation rests on seven years of record with 13% of past items unclassifiable, so treat its per-head counts as approximate.

Finally, work backwards from the mistakes. Every item above whose answer surprised you names a concept to revise, and the ‘Basis’ line under it names the year the examiner last asked it, so you can go to the real question on the relevant topic page and see how it was actually worded.
